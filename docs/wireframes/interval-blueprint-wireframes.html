1Product vision

Interval is a personal engineering learning platform that turns the small gaps in a developer’s day into real learning. It finds existing high-quality technical content, connects it to the user’s interests, projects and current knowledge, and helps them make steady progress across many short sessions. A game layer lets them practice, prove and compete on what they’ve learned.

It’s built for people who want to learn but drift to Instagram, YouTube or random browsing when they have a few spare minutes. Above all, it’s for developers waiting on AI coding agents like Claude Code, Codex or Cursor to finish their work.

Core principle. Interval doesn’t generate its own knowledge library. It discovers, organizes, personalizes and connects knowledge that already exists. AI is used for discovery, ranking, relevance notes, supplementary explanations, scoring and question generation. Original authors stay the source of truth, and AI-generated material is always labeled.

Four pillars

  1. Discover: relevant articles, case studies and books from trusted sources.
  2. Learn continuously: persistent paths through books and topics, sized to the time available.
  3. Personalize: adapt to interests, level, projects, time, history and format.
  4. Practice: quick games and system design battles that test and reinforce what was learned.

Final product definition

A personalized engineering learning platform that brings together existing technical content, ties discovery to each user’s knowledge and projects, delivers learning in time-sized moments or full articles, and reinforces it through competitive practice. It’s available on mobile, on the web, and right beside the developer’s coding agent.

The defining experience stays simple. Whenever a developer has a few spare minutes, they can find something worth learning, or a quick game that tests it, and pick up where they left off next time.

3Foundations

Two foundations shape every feature in the product.

3.1 The concept graph

The concept graph is Interval’s core data model. Concepts are specific technical ideas such as replication lag, connection pooling, consistent hashing, B-tree indexes or leader election. They link to each other through relationships like prerequisite of, related to and applied in.

  • Projects: a user’s projects map to concepts through their technologies and challenges. FastAPI plus PostgreSQL maps to connection pooling, transaction isolation and indexing.
  • Learner state: each user has a state for each concept. Reading, notes and game performance all update it.
  • Questions: every game question tests one or more concepts.

Because of this, recommendations, “why this matters to you” notes, prerequisites, the review queue, path checkpoints, game question selection and the knowledge map are all views of the same graph rather than separate systems. Interval can tell a user exactly where the edge of their knowledge is and what to learn next.

3.2 Learning moments and whole articles

The feed offers both formats, and the user’s time budget decides which appears.

A learning moment is a self-contained section of an article or book, sized to fit the time available, with a clear payoff and a “continue from here” pointer. A 3-minute budget surfaces moments (“Section 2 of 4 · 3 min”). A longer budget, or opening a card directly, surfaces the full article. Progress tracks at the moment level either way, so a moment can grow into a full read, and a half-read article resumes as its next moment.

For web articles, a moment is a pointer into the publisher’s original page (a heading anchor or a scroll position) plus metadata. It is never a republished copy. Splitting content into sections uses only what the source allows: headings and structure where parsing is permitted, and full section boundaries for books and documents the user is authorized to process.

5Learning features

A. Onboarding and learner profile

Onboarding takes under a minute, in three short steps. Users pick topics, a familiarity level, a typical break length (1–3, 3–8 or 8–15 minutes), and preferred formats: read, diagrams, listen or play. Connecting GitHub is optional and imports permitted repository metadata to build project context. Students can verify their university with a college email address for leaderboards and tournaments.

The profile evolves from behavior, but explicit choices always win. Inferred interests never silently override what the user set, and users can view, edit or reset any inferred interest.

B. Content discovery and sources

Sources include technical publications like ByteByteGo, developer communities like DEV, independent and company engineering blogs, postmortems and Medium writing, Devpost project write-ups, newsletters and conference talks, and books and long-form material the user is authorized to access.

Ingestion uses RSS and Atom feeds, publisher-approved APIs and permitted crawling. The source system handles subscriptions, automatic detection of new posts, metadata (title, author, date, canonical URL, reading time, access type), duplicate detection across sites and newsletters, quality and credibility signals, freshness and broken-link checks, and paywall awareness. Users can follow, mute, unfollow and prioritize any source.

C. Personalized feed

The feed is ranked for relevance, usefulness, quality, novelty and the user’s current learning journey, not popularity alone. It mixes familiar interests with adjacent concepts just past the edge of the user’s graph.

  • Learning moments and whole articles, each marked with whether it fits the chosen time.
  • Continue cards for unfinished reading and paths.
  • The daily challenge card.
  • “Test yourself” prompts after finished reading.
  • When agent hooks are on, a banner with the agent’s estimated time left.

Every card shows why it was recommended. Save, Test 60s and Why this? stay on the card. More like this, Less like this, Not interested and Hide source sit in its overflow menu. Filters cover topic, author, source, difficulty, format and time.

D. In-app browser

Web articles open in a LinkedIn-style in-app browser that shows the publisher’s page untouched, with its branding and attribution intact. A back arrow returns to the feed, and Open original website is always available. A section rail shows where the current moment sits in the article.

A bottom sheet holds Interval’s context without altering the page. It starts as a one-line peek (the “why this matters to you” note plus Save, Note, Listen and Test 60s) and expands at the end of a section to offer one next step: test yourself or continue to the next section.

If a site blocks embedding, needs external sign-in, or doesn’t work in a web view, the app offers the system browser. On the web app, where most publishers block embedding, articles open in a new tab and the context panel stays on Interval’s side, with a Mark section done control.

E. “Why this matters to you”

Each item can carry a short, personalized note generated from the concept graph and the user’s project context. For example: “Your billing-api runs FastAPI on PostgreSQL. This is the limit you’ll hit first.” These notes are labeled as AI-generated, never rewrite the author’s content, and can include grounded explanations of unfamiliar terms.

F. Books, paths and checkpoints

Users add books and documents three ways: a supported ebook integration, a PDF upload of material they’re authorized to use, or a URL. After a book is added, Interval detects the title, author and table of contents; finds natural section boundaries and splits them into short sessions; saves position, with progress tracked by chapter, section and concept; links related articles and case studies to the book’s concepts; and supports notes, highlights and labeled AI explanations.

Paths can span books, articles and case studies on a topic. Users can pause, reorder, skip or change a path from the Edit control in the Paths header.

Checkpoints sit between path sections and appear as their own steps on the path timeline, with the pass mark and what they unlock. A checkpoint is a 60-second quiz built from the section’s concepts. Passing it marks those concepts as practiced and unlocks the next section, and missed concepts go to the review queue. Checkpoints use the same engine as quick games.

Interval never invents chapter boundaries or claims to reproduce a book’s structure without authorized access. DRM-protected books work only through supported integrations, and a user’s subscription doesn’t grant permission to republish text.

G. Audio

Audio runs from the global mini-player, which expands into a full player. There are two modes:

  • Narrate source: reads eligible text aloud.
  • Smart explain: an audio-first explanation with short and detailed depths, labeled as AI under the mode switch.

The player supports speed control, skipping, a sleep timer, background playback, lock-screen controls, resume, a queue, and transitions between connected concepts. Explanations of diagrams and code are voice-friendly. Narrated source and AI explanation are always clearly distinguished. Full-text audio is limited to material Interval is permitted to process, such as user-uploaded files, openly licensed content and partner content.

H. Project-aware recommendations

Users add projects manually or through GitHub with explicit permission. Projects start as a simple profile of technologies and goals that maps onto the concept graph. Reading code is separate and only happens if the user explicitly enables it. Users can update or remove project context at any time.

I. Library, review and knowledge map

The library holds saved items, notes, highlights, history, collections and the audio queue. The review queue sits at the top, pulling from concepts that are due for review, missed game rounds and failed checkpoints, with a count and a time estimate. The knowledge map shows the user’s slice of the concept graph and how it grows over time, with states drawn as distinct shapes and a legend. Notes and links are exportable. Saving an item keeps its URL and attribution, not the full text.

J. Search and exploration

Search covers indexed metadata and permitted content by topic, author, publication, technology, concept and project relevance. A concept match comes first, showing the user’s state and every format that covers it. Users can follow topic spaces, ask for an explanation at a chosen difficulty level, and explore one concept across multiple sources. Answers always point back to original material rather than hiding it behind an AI summary.

K. Notifications and digests

All notifications are opt-in: daily or weekly digests that combine sources into one message, new posts from followed authors, path continuation reminders, and daily challenge and tournament reminders. Users control quiet hours, frequency and channels.

L. Adaptive learning

The system learns from meaningful signals: completion, saves, skips, replays, explicit feedback, checkpoint results and game performance. It doesn’t just count clicks. It adjusts difficulty and topic mix, avoids repeats, brings important concepts back on a spaced schedule, and measures recall and continuity rather than screen time.

6Game layer

6.1 Role and entry points

Play is its own layer with its own tab. The rest of the product also leads into it:

  • Feed: the daily challenge card, and “test yourself (60s)” after finishing an article or moment.
  • In-app browser: the Test 60s action in the article sheet.
  • Paths: checkpoint quizzes between sections.
  • Desktop companion and IDE extension: a quick game while an agent runs.
  • Play tab: deliberate play, leagues, battles and leaderboards, with one recommended game drawn from concepts due for review.
Every game ends by sending the user back to learning. Missed concepts become feed items, path resumes or review-queue entries. Results screens make the reading for a missed concept the main action. Game results update the concept graph, so practice becomes the measure of what the user actually learned.

Because AI league players bring different tools, its rankings measure the person plus their tools, not the person alone. Problems are designed so that raw AI output fails without human judgment: requirements need clarifying, designs need to survive failure injection, and extension rounds change the rules mid-match. A one-line rule under the league switch says what each league measures.

6.3 Quick games

Quick games take 1 to 3 minutes and work on every surface.

Question types include multiple choice; back-of-envelope estimation such as QPS, storage or bandwidth; picking the right component for a scenario; spotting the bottleneck in a small diagram; ranking latencies; and consistency and trade-off calls. Questions are drawn from the user’s concepts and adjacent ones. In solo modes each answer gets immediate feedback, and estimation misses show the expected value.

6.4 HLD battles

HLD battles are desktop-only and last about 30 minutes. They come in three formats: live 1v1, live 4-player rooms with ranked placements, and scheduled tournaments, including university-vs-university brackets.

The canvas. Players build designs from components: load balancers, app servers, caches, databases with replicas or shards, queues, CDNs and workers. They configure each one, choosing replication mode, failover strategy, shard key, cache policy and so on. Each problem defines requirements, such as “100M new URLs a month, 50k redirects per second, p99 under 100 ms, survive a zone outage.”

The simulation. The engine runs the design through baseline load, peak traffic, a cold cache, a primary database failure and a zone outage. It checks capacity, the latency budget and single points of failure. Results are deterministic, so the same design always gets the same result. Each failed scenario shows a one-line reason, and the failing component is marked on the canvas.

The simulation acts as a gate. A design that fails the baseline scenario earns no trade-off points, so good writing can’t rescue a broken design. The battle screen states the gate and whether it has been met.

AI rubric safeguards

  • Short, specific justifications: players attach a short “why this choice” note to each decision, rather than writing one long essay.
  • Answers as data: the judge treats every submission strictly as content to evaluate. Length limits apply, and anything that looks like instructions is stripped out.
  • Repeat judging: each submission is scored three times, and the median counts.
  • Transparent results: results show each rubric item with a one-line reason and a way to dispute it.

After the match, an AI coach explains what a stronger design would have done. This feedback is labeled as AI and never affects ratings.

6.5 LLD battles

LLD battles are desktop-only and last about 45 minutes. They run live 1v1 and in scheduled tournaments.

Fixed interfaces. Each problem specifies exact class and method signatures (for example, park(vehicle) → Ticket), so hidden tests can run against any submission. Code runs in a sandbox that supports multiple languages.

Test layers: visible tests, hidden tests, concurrency tests (such as 100 parallel calls that must never hand out the same parking spot twice), and extension-round tests. Failing tests show the input that broke them.

Extension rounds. Partway through, the requirements change (“add EV charging spots”) and new tests unlock. Extensible designs adapt quickly, while messy code breaks. This rewards good design without subjective grading.

Scoring: tests passed, with time to reach that count as the tiebreaker. An optional AI code-quality review appears after the match and doesn’t affect ratings.

6.6 Matchmaking and ratings

  • Ratings: each league and mode family has its own rating, using a Glicko-style system.
  • Matching: players are matched within a rating band, and the band widens over time.
  • Bot fallback: if no live opponent appears within about 15 seconds, the player faces a rated bot at their level. A countdown shows when this happens, and players can skip the wait. This solves the early problem of too few live players.
  • Fairness: both players get the same questions, and the server controls all timing.
  • Friend challenges: players can challenge someone directly through a shareable link.
  • Seasons: ratings run in seasons, with soft resets between them.

6.7 Leaderboards

Leaderboards rank players globally, by country and by university. University rankings require verification through a college email address. Players can filter by league, mode and season, and see friends’ rankings. Each player sees their own rank and percentile pinned below the top of the board, and their university’s standing among universities.

6.8 Question bank

AI-generated, human-reviewed

Generated from the concept graph and reviewed before going live. Fills the everyday pool for quick games, daily challenges and checkpoints.

Community-submitted

Written by users, moderated, rated by players and weighted by contributor reputation. Adds variety and real-world scenarios.

Hand-written

Reserved for tournaments and ranked seasons, where leaked or low-quality questions would do the most damage. Rotated and retired after use.

Every question is tagged with concepts, difficulty and source. Difficulty is recalibrated automatically from real response data. Questions that players frequently dispute, or that behave unusually, are pulled for review.

6.9 Results and the learning loop

Results show the outcome and rating change; accuracy and average answer time; the score breakdown, plus the rubric for battles; the AI coach’s notes; and concepts to revisit. Each missed concept links to a specific article, moment or path section, labeled “opens in your feed,” and the main button opens that reading. Players can watch battle replays and spectate top matches.

6.10 Fair play

Human league. Detection signals include pasted text, focus changes, typing patterns and sudden rating jumps. Flags go to review rather than triggering automatic bans, and penalties hit ratings rather than accounts. Time pressure and extension rounds are the main defense, and players are told honestly that detection isn’t perfect.

AI league. There are no tool restrictions, so detection isn’t needed.

6.11 Mobile and desktop split

Mobile covers quick games, daily challenges, checkpoints, leaderboards, battle replays, spectating, tournament registration, and a handoff for battles (send a link to desktop now, or set a reminder for later). Desktop covers everything, including the HLD canvas and LLD editor.

7Agent-waiting surfaces

This is the use case that gives Interval its identity, and it’s part of the final product.

Desktop companion

A menubar window that shows the agent’s estimated runtime when known and offers options sorted to fit the gap: a 60-second sprint, the daily challenge, resuming a path, or a time-sized moment. When the agent finishes, it saves the user’s place and offers one button back to their code.

IDE extension

The same quick-session options inside the editor, without switching windows.

Coding-agent hooks

Opt-in integrations with tools like Claude Code and Cursor that report when a long task starts and finishes. With separate explicit permission, the topic of the agent’s current task can shape what’s suggested. If the agent is working on auth, an OAuth moment might appear.

No dependency

The product never depends on these signals. Everything works whenever the user simply has a few spare minutes.

Core data entities

Learning

  • User: preferences, goals, settings, verified university.
  • Project: technologies, goals, authorized context.
  • Source: website, publication, author, feed or newsletter.
  • Content item: canonical URL, metadata, permitted content, access rights.
  • Moment: a section pointer within a content item, with estimated time and concepts.
  • Concept and relationship: the nodes and edges of the graph.
  • Learner concept state: unseen, explored, practiced, mastered or due, with a review schedule.
  • Learning path and checkpoint: an ordered or adaptive sequence with gating quizzes.
  • Progress: reading and listening position, completion.
  • Annotation: notes, highlights, bookmarks.
  • Subscription, feedback, recommendation.

Games

  • Question: concepts, difficulty, type, source, review status.
  • Game mode, match, round and submission.
  • Design submission: the component graph and its configuration.
  • Simulation run: per-scenario results.
  • Test run: results per test layer.
  • Rubric score: per item, median of three judgments.
  • League, rating, season, leaderboard entry.
  • Tournament and bracket.
  • University affiliation.
  • Fair-play flag.

Real-time and infrastructure notes

  • Live matches: need a real-time connection (WebSockets) with server-controlled timers.
  • Sandboxes: the simulation engine and test runner run in isolated sandboxes, with resource limits for each submission.
  • Cost controls: apply to AI tagging, embeddings, audio generation, question generation and rubric judging.

10Content rights, privacy, fairness and AI boundaries

Content rights

  • The original publisher stays the source of truth.
  • A web view or moment pointer is not republication.
  • Full text is processed only where permitted.
  • Subscription access is not redistribution permission.
  • DRM is never bypassed.
  • Content-removal and licensing requests are handled through admin tools.

Labeling

Original material, external references, AI explanations, AI notes and AI coach output are always visually distinct.

Original textAI explanation

A solid badge marks original text. A dashed outline and label mark AI content.

Privacy

  • Every integration (GitHub, ebooks, agent hooks) uses fine-grained, revocable permissions.
  • Reading code and seeing an agent’s current task each require separate opt-in, shown as separate switches in Profile.
  • Users can export or delete their data and reset inferred interests.
  • University verification stores only the affiliation, not the email contents.

Fairness

  • League rules are stated clearly.
  • Detection in the human league leads to review before any penalty.
  • Players can see why they won or lost through the rubric, and dispute items.
  • AI scoring never exceeds its 25-point share and only applies to designs that pass the simulation gate.

12Open decisions before phasing

  1. Business model. Not yet included. The options were individual plans, team plans and publisher partnerships. This decision affects licensing for audio and books.
  2. Fidelity of the simulation engine. How realistic the capacity and latency models need to be for competitive play, and how to calibrate them.
  3. Source policy for moments. Which sources allow section-level parsing, and what to do when they don’t (fall back to whole articles only).
  4. University verification outside email domains. Handling schools without dedicated student email.
  5. LLD 4-player rooms. Whether LLD also gets rooms or stays live 1v1 plus tournaments.
  6. Language support for LLD. Which languages are supported at launch.
  7. Hook support for coding agents. Which agents expose usable start and finish signals.
  8. Product name. “Interval” is used throughout the designs as a working name.
